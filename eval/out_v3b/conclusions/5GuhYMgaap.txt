The paper’s central contribution is the SolverLearner framework: a two-stage procedure in which an LLM first induces an explicit mapping function from few-shot examples (“Function Proposal”) and then an external interpreter applies this function to new inputs (“Function Execution”) so that inductive reasoning is attributed to the LLM and deductive execution is offloaded. However, this core mechanism is already present in prior work. Chen et al. (2022) Program-of-Thoughts prompting, Hypothesis Search: Inductive Reasoning with Language Models, and Logic-LM (Pan et al., 2023) all require the LLM to generate programmatic hypotheses/solvers that encode the induced rule and delegate execution to an external engine, explicitly disentangling induction from deterministic computation; SolverLearner mainly repackages this as a named two-step protocol and casts it as an “inductive vs deductive” framing. Similarly, the empirical claim that LLMs are strong at proposing rules/functions but weak at applying them—especially on counterfactual variants—is substantially prefigured by “Phenomenal Yet Puzzling: Testing Inductive Reasoning Capabilities of Language Models with Hypothesis Refinement,” which reports the same qualitative pattern under a very similar LM-as-hypothesis-proposer + interpreter setup. The additional formalization and comparative experiments are incremental relative to these works. Overall, the central contribution is not novel enough to constitute a genuine advance.
